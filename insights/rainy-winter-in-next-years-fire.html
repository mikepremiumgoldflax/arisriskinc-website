The rainy winter is already in next year's fire

Weather and terrain get the attention when a wildfire runs. Fuel gets less, because it does its work quietly and months in advance. A wet season grows vegetation; a dry season kills and cures it into a continuous bed. By the time ignition arrives, the load has been set for weeks. That timing is where coarse hazard scores go blind, and where a physics model has to earn its keep.

Fuel is a timing problem

A fire needs fuel, weather and topography to move — what foresters call the fire behavior triangle. Weather and terrain draw the headlines. Fuel is the leg that loads on a schedule most risk scores never watch. A wet period grows vegetation; a dry period kills and cures it; months later a careless spark finds a continuous bed waiting.

Spain shows the pattern cleanly. January 2026 was the wettest January in a quarter century, and in the area later burned by the Avila fire rainfall ran 175% above normal, feeding a heavy accumulation of biomass. That fuel went on to carry the largest and most destructive fire in Spain's recorded history, roughly 44,000 hectares, after a negligent spark from heavy machinery.

Spokane told the same story in a different dialect. A snow drought dried forests weeks early, spring rain grew grasses and shrubs that summer heat cured into a continuous bed, and a dry cold front did the rest. More than 800 homes and structures were lost.

Structure beats coverage

Fuel is not only how much vegetation sits on the ground; it is how that vegetation is arranged. Near Bordeaux, the Gironde fire ran through intensively managed maritime pine with needle litter, ladder fuels linking ground to canopy, and connected crowns that let fire jump tree to tree — about 40,000 hectares. The same biomass in a different arrangement burns differently. And moisture decides whether it burns at all: dry fuel burns faster and hotter because the heat does not have to boil off water first.

That is the gap in a hazard band. It tells you a neighborhood sits in a dangerous zone. It does not tell you how fuel is arranged around one specific home, how that structure will respond, or how a season of rain-then-drought changed the load since the map was drawn. Meanwhile the thing being protected keeps moving into the fire. The US added roughly 15 million homes to the wildland-urban interface between 1990 and 2020, a near-50% increase, and home survivability inside large-fire perimeters fell 12% over two decades.

Most models are judged on history. We also grade ours forward.

The money follows the physics

The cost is landing on every bill. California's insurer of last resort has sought a rate increase of nearly 36%. Using an open parcel-level dataset, researchers estimate climate change will push statewide annual wildfire losses to owner-occupied homes up 85% by 2047. When the number is that large, the difference between a coarse band and a building-by-building answer stops being academic. It decides which policies you keep and what you charge to keep them.

The fuel load for next year is being set now, in the rain. The honest way to price it is to model the physics, then put your number on the record.
