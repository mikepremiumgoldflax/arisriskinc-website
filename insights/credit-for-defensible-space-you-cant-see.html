MitigationOctober 2026

Credit for defensible space you can't actually see

California has decided that mitigation should move price. Regulators now require insurers to discount homeowners who harden their homes and clear defensible space. It is a sound instinct and a long-overdue one. But a discount is only as honest as the model behind it, and most of the models carriers rely on cannot actually see the thing they are being asked to reward. If you price the ZIP code, you cannot credit the parcel. And if you cannot credit the parcel, the whole incentive loop quietly breaks.

The mandate is now explicit

The pressure on California homeowners is not abstract. Under the state's insurer of last resort, rates rose an average of 29.1% beginning October 15, and the FAIR Plan had swelled to 696,562 policies with $768 billion of exposure by June. Voluntary-market premiums have climbed too, up 84% since the end of 2020 by one Stanford estimate. Against that backdrop, policy has turned toward mitigation: AB 1 (Connolly, 2025) requires the Department of Insurance to keep updating regulations that make insurers offer discounts to homeowners who invest in risk reduction.

The logic is good. Make the premium reflect the home, and the homeowner has a reason to spend on defensible space and a hardened roof. You can see it work at the level of a single property. One Napa County owner documented his home-hardening, left a FAIR Plan premium approaching $10,000, and secured voluntary coverage at less than half the cost. Carriers are responding in kind, with some building their own checklists for what counts as a wildfire-prepared home.

A discount you can't resolve is a discount you can't defend

Here is the problem. A mitigation credit is a claim about one building. The roof is Class A or it is not. The five feet around the foundation are clear or they are not. That is parcel-level reality. A model built on ZIP or county aggregates cannot represent it. It assigns the hardened home and the untouched home next door the same score because it only knows the polygon they share. When the credit is bolted onto that kind of model, it becomes a flat adjustment applied to a neighborhood, not a measurement of a home.

The evidence that this is already happening is in the record. Research reviewed by the Terner Center finds that existing California wildfire discounts have been inconsistently applied and insufficient in magnitude. That is what you get when the regulatory ask outruns the modeling resolution. The mandate says reward mitigation; the model can only approximate location.

If two neighbors pay the same premium while one has done the work and the other has not, the carrier has not priced risk. It has subsidized the free rider and taxed the person who spent the money.

The science does not live at the ZIP level either. Fire behavior is driven by fuel, terrain, wind, and the condition of the specific parcel. Researchers advancing real-time spread models have found that even the ignition time and local terrain materially change the forecast. If when and where a fire starts reshapes its path, then the hundred meters around a structure is not noise to be averaged away. It is the signal. The direction of travel in the data world confirms it: the first open-source, parcel-level dataset of wildfire risk to U.S. buildings now exists, and the same analysis projects statewide annual expected losses to owner-occupied homes rising 85% by 2047. The stakes are large and growing: one industry report counts 2.5 million western homes and $1.4 trillion of exposure. Pricing that book at neighborhood resolution is how you end up both overcharging mitigators and underpricing the homes that will actually burn.

What resolution has to mean

The mitigation mandate is not a marketing requirement. It is a measurement requirement. To honor it, a model has to see the parcel, represent the physics that governs it, and show its work when a homeowner or a regulator asks why a credit was given or withheld. That is the standard ARIS was built to. Our 2026 California forecast is parcel-level and physics-grounded, resolving fuel, terrain, wind, and defensible space at the property rather than the polygon. It is bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable, so a credit is a defensible number, not a gesture. And because it was sealed and cryptographically timestamped before fire season, reality grades it. A mitigation discount should rest on the same footing: a measurement you can reproduce, not an average you can only hope is close.
