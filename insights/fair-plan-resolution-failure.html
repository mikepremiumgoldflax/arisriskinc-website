The FAIR Plan Is a Resolution Failure With a Balance Sheet

California's insurer of last resort has stopped acting like a backstop and started acting like a primary carrier. That is not a policy accident. It is the arithmetic of coarse resolution playing out on a public balance sheet. When you decide who to insure by drawing lines around neighborhoods instead of measuring individual homes, you sweep the resilient and the doomed into the same pool — and eventually someone has to hold that pool. In California, that someone is the FAIR Plan, and its exposure now runs into the hundreds of billions.

The last resort is now the main event

The scale is no longer a footnote. The number of FAIR Plan policies climbed 44% in 2025 to roughly 668,600, while total exposure reached about $768 billion against cash reserves of only around $200 million to $400 million. That is a mismatch of three orders of magnitude between what the plan could owe and what it holds. It is not hypothetical: the January 2025 Los Angeles-area fires were expected to produce about $4 billion in FAIR Plan losses, prompting a $1 billion assessment on member insurers. Rates are following the exposure. New and renewed policies will be priced under an approved 29.1% average statewide increase starting October 15, and the burden is not spread evenly.

The pool grew because private carriers retreated. Moody's counts the non-renewal of more than one million wildfire policies in recent years, with FAIR Plan exposure growing by over 50% in Los Angeles County alone between 2024 and 2025. Statewide, a June 2026 Stanford University study found homeowners insurance premiums have risen 84% since 2020. Those are the symptoms. The disease is resolution.

Coarse geography manufactures the pool

Here is the mechanism. A carrier that evaluates risk at ZIP or county granularity cannot distinguish the hardened home with clear defensible space from the ember trap next door. Faced with a book it cannot price, it does the only rational thing coarse data permits: it exits the whole area. Every home in the polygon gets the same verdict, and the resilient ones land in the FAIR Plan alongside the vulnerable. The plan inherits a portfolio it also cannot separate, so it raises rates across the board — steepest, as expected, in fire-prone canyons and foothills.

The waste in this is enormous, and it is measurable. Property-level analysis from Cotality found that homes in the top 10% of its mitigation score carry expected losses roughly 78% below the statewide average, while the bottom 10% runs more than ten times that average — about $47 in expected loss for every $1 on the best-prepared homes. A spread of that magnitude lives inside the same neighborhoods carriers are abandoning wholesale. The same work showed that layering conflagration potential onto traditional scoring can raise a property's risk rating by up to 40 points, pushing meaningful hazard deep into neighborhoods legacy maps previously classified as low risk. The coarse map is wrong in both directions at once.

When your smallest unit of judgment is a neighborhood, you cannot reward the homeowner who cleared brush and re-roofed. You can only price the average — and the average is a fiction that dumps good risk into a public pool and lets bad risk hide in it.

Resolution is the fix, not the rate filing

The exposure is not diffuse. Cotality counts over 2.5 million properties across the ten most exposed western states with moderate or greater wildfire risk, a combined reconstruction cost of nearly $1.4 trillion, with California alone holding 1.28 million at-risk properties worth $850 billion. You do not underwrite a trillion dollars of concentrated risk with a crayon. The reason carriers keep coarse portfolios and coarse portfolios keep feeding the FAIR Plan is that the resolution of the risk signal has never matched the resolution of the decision. Underwriting is a parcel-level act. Modeling it at the county level guarantees the exodus.

Resolution is what fixes it.
