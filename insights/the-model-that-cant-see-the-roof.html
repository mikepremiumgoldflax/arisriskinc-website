The model that can't see the roof

California just rebuilt its insurance market around a single idea: reward the homeowner who reduces risk. The problem is that the models blessed to price that risk operate at a scale that cannot see the very things the rules are paying for.

California spent the last year rewiring its home insurance market around one premise: carriers should be allowed to look forward, and homeowners who reduce risk should pay less. Mercury was the first carrier to file rates under Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara's new rules, which let insurers use forward-looking catastrophe models and pass some reinsurance costs to consumers in exchange for writing more policies in wildfire-distressed areas. Its new rates took effect this month and commit the company to thousands of additional policies over two years. The bargain is explicit: harden your home, get access and discounts.

The incentive is parcel-level. The model isn't.

Mercury's minimum standard reads like a parcel-by-parcel checklist: a Class A roof, dual-pane windows, non-combustible material on the first six inches of siding, ember-resistant vents, and a true zone zero. Do more and you earn deeper discounts. That is the right science. Ignition at the wildland-urban interface is overwhelmingly a function of what happens in the last few feet — roof, vents, siding, and the space immediately around the structure.

Here is the problem. The catastrophe models being blessed to price this risk do not operate at that scale. The company's own community resilience manager said it plainly: their catastrophe models can see a big scale, but they cannot see down at the granular level to what homeowners are doing. It took bringing underwriters physically to Paradise to register mitigation the model could not. That is not a knock on one carrier — it is a description of the tool.

A regulator can require carriers to reward home hardening. It cannot require a coarse model to resolve the roof it is being asked to price.

The market is already voting with hardware

You can measure the resolution gap by what people are building to close it. RockRose Risk launched a lidar-equipped assessment rover explicitly to capture ground-level conditions that satellite imagery and ZIP-code models can't see. When a firm sends a robot down a driveway, it is conceding that the view from orbit and from postal geography misses the variables that decide the claim.

The stakes are not academic. California is the nation's largest home insurance market, worth roughly $15 billion, and insurers had already withdrawn, non-renewed, and raised rates before the 2025 Los Angeles fires left thousands of homeowners questioning whether they had adequate coverage. The physical drivers have not relented: PG&E warned of required power shutoffs across Monterey, San Benito and San Luis Obispo counties this month as red flag warnings flew for high wind, low humidity and heat. And the fires themselves are hotter, less predictable, and greater in number, pushing into the wildland-urban interface where the home becomes the fuel.

That last phrase matters for pricing. Conflagration — fire traveling home to home, as it did in the Marshall Fire, Altadena, and the Palisades — is a parcel-adjacency phenomenon. Whether your insured survives depends not only on its own hardening but on the structure a few feet away. A model that resolves ZIP codes or counties cannot represent that geometry at all. It averages away the exact signal the new rules are trying to price.

What actually closes the gap

If the regulatory incentive is granular, the risk estimate has to be granular too, or the two will drift apart and the discounts will land on the wrong homes. That means resolving risk at the parcel — the specific roof, the specific vents, the defensible space, the neighbor — and grounding it in fire physics: fuel, terrain, wind, and the space around the structure, not a smoothed regional curve.

It also means being honest about accuracy. A model that scores this market should be reproducible bit-for-bit and auditable for data leakage, and its forecast should be committed in advance and graded by reality rather than tuned after the fact. That is the standard ARIS holds itself to: a sealed, cryptographically timestamped 2026 California forecast, built at parcel resolution on physics rather than postal geography, with no insurance written and no channel conflict to protect. California has already written the incentive. The only question left is whether the risk estimate can see what the incentive is paying for.
