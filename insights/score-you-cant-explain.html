The score you can't explain is the score you can't defend

A wildfire risk score used to be an internal number. In a growing number of states it is becoming something a homeowner can demand you explain and a regulator can demand you defend. That quietly changes what a model has to be able to do.

The score just became a legal document

On July 1, 2026, Colorado's HB25-1182 took effect, requiring property insurers to be transparent about the wildfire risk models they use and to let property owners appeal their scores. The written notice a carrier sends at application, renewal, or nonrenewal must carry a plain-language explanation of the score, the range of scores a property could receive, and the impact each mitigation action could have on it.

Read that as an engineering requirement, not a compliance chore. To satisfy it, a model has to say why a specific parcel scored the way it did, what would move the number, and by how much. And it has to do so on a clock: the insurer must acknowledge an appeal within 10 calendar days and return a reconsidered decision within 30, with the Commissioner able to request the file if the appeal is denied.

Colorado is less an outlier than a preview. Homeowners' insurance rates in the state roughly doubled from 2020 to 2025, and the Marshall Fire destroyed hundreds of homes in communities that later watched premiums climb. When prices move that fast, the political demand for an explainable, contestable number follows. Other states will copy the mechanism.

Opacity is now a liability, not a moat

For years, model opacity was treated as protection: proprietary, hard to second-guess. Under an appeal regime it flips. A score you cannot decompose to the parcel is a score you cannot defend when a homeowner arrives with photos of cleared brush and a hardened roof. "We used a vendor model" is not a reconsideration.

This is where ZIP and county resolution breaks. Coarse geography assigns two neighbors the same number even when one has done everything right and the other nothing. The Colorado law explicitly requires insurers to consider both parcel-level and community-wide mitigation, or to grant discounts to owners who can demonstrate it. A model that only knows the ZIP cannot honor that, because the thing being appealed, the work done on one specific property, is invisible to it.

The stakes of coarse geography are not abstract. In California, nine ZIP codes out of more than 1,700 account for about 7% of the FAIR Plan's liability exposure, or $44 billion as of September 2025.

When that much money rides on lines drawn around postal boundaries, the difference between a ZIP average and the actual parcel is the whole game. The better vendors see it. ZestyAI, describing its own property tools, frames the shift as an industry moving "toward a more exact understanding of risk – grounded in what's actually present at each property." That is the right direction. The open question for any model is whether "what's actually present" can be reconstructed, explained, and defended line by line when challenged.

Explainability has to be built in, not bolted on

You cannot retrofit explainability onto a black box after a homeowner files. Either the model reasons about the physical drivers of fire at the parcel, fuel, terrain, wind, defensible space, or it reasons about correlations that happen to track geography, and those correlations cannot answer "what would clearing this brush do to my score."

This is the case ARIS was built to make. Our forecasts run at parcel resolution on physics-grounded fire science, so a score maps to physical drivers a homeowner and a regulator can see and act on. Every run is bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable, which means a specific parcel's number can be reconstructed and defended on the record, not approximated after the fact. And because we sell data and do not write insurance, no channel conflict shapes the score. In a world where the score is now a legal document, being able to explain it is not a feature. It is the product.
