The 29% headline is the least useful number in the filing

The California FAIR Plan is raising rates this fall, and the average increase will lead every headline. It is the least useful number in the entire filing. An average is a summary statistic, and a wildfire book is precisely the thing you cannot price on a summary statistic. The story is in the spread, and the spread is where most models quietly fail.

The average is the wrong unit

Start with what the headline conceals. The FAIR Plan's 29.1% increase takes effect October 15, but that figure describes almost no actual policyholder. About half of customers will see increases of 30% to 50%, a quarter will see decreases of as much as 80%, and the remaining quarter will land below 30% or between 50% and 200% depending on the property. The dispersion is concrete: in Grizzly Flats, roughly 150 customers go from an average of $2,671 to $5,485, the ZIP covering Orinda rises about 31% to nearly $7,000, and most San Francisco policyholders see rates fall in the same filing.

The plan is explicit about the driver. As its spokesperson put it, the largest component of the increase relates to the wildfire portion of the premium, so properties at significant wildfire risk absorb the increase while lower-risk properties get relief. In other words, the entire exercise is an act of separation. The average is just the residue left over after you sort the book.

Dispersion has to be earned

The reason this dispersion is now legal is the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, which lets insurers price on forward-looking risk models rather than historical data alone. California had been the only state that barred that approach, forcing carriers to use backward-looking data that is increasingly worthless as climate-driven disasters intensify. Forward-looking pricing is the right call. But a forward-looking model earns the right to charge one neighbor more and another less only if it can actually tell the two apart.

The magnitudes make the point. Cotality's own work finds that homes in the top decile of mitigation carry expected losses roughly 78% below the statewide average, while the bottom decile runs more than ten times that average — about $47 of expected loss for every $1 on the best-prepared homes. And layering conflagration potential onto a conventional score can add as much as 40 points, pushing real hazard into neighborhoods legacy maps had classified as low risk. A 47-to-1 spread inside a single book is not noise to be smoothed into an average. It is the signal.

If two parcels on the same street carry expected losses that differ by an order of magnitude, then any model that assigns them the same rate is not conservative. It is wrong twice.

Resolution is the binding constraint

This is where the machinery matters. A model built on ZIP or county aggregates cannot produce a defensible minus-80-to-plus-200 spread, because it does not resolve the thing that creates the spread: the specific parcel, its fuel, its slope, its defensible space, its exposure to embers and to the house next door. When a regulator, a reinsurer, or a policyholder's attorney asks why this home was charged and that one credited, an aggregate model has no answer that survives contact. It priced a polygon, not a property.

Physics is what makes the distinctions legible. Researchers combining satellite observation with terrain and fire dynamics have shown that even ignition timing and slope materially change how a fire spreads and where it goes. The same variables that govern spread govern loss, and they operate at the scale of the parcel, not the postal code.
