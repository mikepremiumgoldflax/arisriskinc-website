Last spring's rain is this summer's fuel

The most dangerous number in a wildfire book this year is not last season's loss ratio. It is how much it rained last spring. Fuel is not a fixed feature of a parcel that you can look up once and forget. It grows in the wet months and cures in the hot ones, and the risk it creates swings hard from year to year on the same piece of ground. Models that treat vegetation as a static layer, and that learn only from the past, are structurally blind to the mechanism that is driving this summer's fires.

Fuel is a verb, not a noun

A parcel's fuel load is the product of two seasons pulling against each other: growth, then curing. This year's European fire season is the cleanest demonstration in recent memory. Fires had burned 28,000 hectares in France and 50,000 hectares in Spain as of 1 July, more than double the average for that time of year. The trigger was not heat alone. A wet winter and spring grew the fuel, and consecutive heatwaves cured it.

The physical record backs this up. In Spain, surface soil moisture ran above the seasonal average from March to May, and the same wet-then-hot pattern set up the prior record season, where high vegetation water content first suppressed fire potential and was then lost during long heatwaves, leaving behind an extensive fuel surplus. Julia Miller of the WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research stated the mechanism plainly.

If a period of active vegetation growth is followed by a period of drought and heat, vegetation becomes stressed and transforms into flammable wildfire fuel.

The corollary is where risk actually lives. As Miller put it, in most of Europe there is enough vegetation to burn, and the critical question is when that vegetation becomes dry enough to burn. A fire prevention scientist on the same report was blunter: climate cannot provoke fires if there is no plant fuel, so fuel availability is a critical factor underlying extreme fires.

This is not a European curiosity, and it is not confined to the forested West. Heatwaves this year have dried out vegetation into ready fuel far outside the traditional fire map: wildfires have burned nearly 204,000 acres in Florida so far in 2026, and Oklahoma has seen a rise in grassland megafires since 2016 as heat and drought load the landscape. Grass and brush cure faster than timber, which means the growth-and-cure swing is even more violent in the places underwriters have historically treated as low-hazard.

History is the wrong teacher

If fuel is dynamic, then a model calibrated to the historical loss record is calibrated to a world that no longer produces the same fires. This is the quiet convergence between catastrophe modeling and attribution science: both need a counterfactual, not a memory. As climate change alters the atmosphere, historical conditions become a less reliable guide to future environmental risks. A natural catastrophe specialist at Swiss Re said it directly: what happened over the past hundred years is not going to repeat over the next 10 years.

That is a problem for any approach that resolves risk to a ZIP code or county and averages away the variation that matters. Fuel state is a parcel-scale variable that changes on a seasonal clock. Two homes a mile apart can sit on opposite sides of a growth-and-cure cycle depending on aspect, soil, prior management, and how the last two rainy seasons landed. A model that reports one number for the whole tract has already thrown away the signal before it starts.

What this means for the book

The right unit of analysis is the parcel, and the right engine is physics, not curve-fitting to old losses. That means modeling the actual drivers of ignition and spread: fuel and its condition, terrain, wind, and defensible space. It means treating this year's fuel as this year's fuel, not last decade's average. It also means being honest about the counterfactual problem rather than hiding it inside a backtest that quietly grades its own homework.
