Mitigation credit is only as real as your resolution

California's Sustainable Insurance Strategy did more than let insurers use forward-looking catastrophe models. It quietly turned mitigation into a pricing variable. A homeowner who clears defensible space and swaps in ember-resistant vents is now supposed to see that work show up in the premium. That is a defensible promise only if your model can see the property. Most can't, and that gap is about to become an underwriting and regulatory liability.

Mitigation is now a rate variable

The direction of travel is clear. The California FAIR Plan is implementing an overall 29.1% rate increase averaged across more than 675,000 policyholders, effective October 15, 2026, with the largest component tied to the wildfire portion of premium and an explicit note that policyholders may qualify for discounts by hardening their home. On the admitted side, Farmers' first filing under the reforms increases the discounts homeowners can get for reducing their wildfire risk, and Travelers has said it will include wildfire-prepared homes that use hardening elements and defensible space. Hardening now flows to price. The question is whether the models being used to set that price can actually distinguish a hardened home from the one next door.

The physics is real, and it is local

The loss-reduction numbers are not marketing. Analysis of roughly 91,800 California home inspections found that homes with well-maintained defensible space were 65% less likely to suffer significant damage, ember-resistant vents cut ignition likelihood by 40%, and combining measures reduced expected losses by up to 70%. Field reconnaissance after the Los Angeles fires told the same story from the other direction: defensible ridgelines and fire-resistant structures served as firebreaks that spared entire neighborhoods, while ember accumulators and connected fuels amplified the destruction. Every one of these features exists at the scale of a single parcel: the vent, the roof, the five feet around the foundation, the slope behind the house.

That is precisely the scale legacy resolution cannot resolve. The same analysis found that neighboring properties within the same wildfire-prone ZIP code can differ by more than fourfold in overall risk, and that roughly 98% of wildfire property damage occurs within about 10% of the state's highest-risk properties.

A model that prices at the ZIP or county level cannot see the 10% that carries the loss, and it cannot see the hardening that moves a home out of it. It can only average. Averaging is the one thing a mitigation credit is designed to defeat.

Credit you can't verify is credit you can't defend

There is a second trap. Even if you decide to reward hardening, whose account of the property do you trust? Research across 38 wildland-urban interface communities found that homeowners generally underestimated their own parcel's wildfire risk relative to trained assessors. Self-reported mitigation is therefore the weakest possible foundation for a rate credit: it is inconsistent, unaudited, and structurally biased toward the discount. A credit built on it will not survive a regulator's review or a bad fire season, and it invites exactly the kind of adverse selection that hollowed out this market in the first place.

So the mitigation era imposes two requirements at once. First, resolution: the model has to operate at the parcel, because that is where fuel, terrain, wind exposure, and defensible space actually live. Second, verifiability: the credit has to rest on physical, reproducible evidence rather than an attestation, and the pipeline has to be auditable so no one can quietly retrofit favorable inputs after the fact.

This is the problem ARIS was built for. Our forecasts are parcel-level and physics-grounded, resolving fuel, terrain, wind, and defensible space at the scale where mitigation changes outcomes rather than smearing them across a ZIP. They are bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable for data leakage, which is what turns a hardening discount from a marketing gesture into a number an actuary can sign and a regulator can inspect. And because ARIS does not write insurance, there is no channel conflict shading which homes get credit. When the rules reward mitigation, the model that can see the mitigation wins. The one that can only average is quietly subsidizing the 10% of parcels that will produce almost all of the loss.
