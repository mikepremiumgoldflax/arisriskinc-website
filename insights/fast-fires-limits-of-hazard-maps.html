Fast fires and the limits of a hazard map

The destruction does not spread out evenly. A small minority of fast-moving fires drives the overwhelming share of homes lost, and that concentration is a statement about fire behavior, not about burn probability. If your risk tool averages hazard over decades, it is measuring the wrong thing for the question an underwriter actually asks: how hard does fire hit this building.

Destruction hides in the tail

Fires are not interchangeable. Work using satellite imagery across the contiguous United States found that "fast fires," those burning more than 4,000 acres in a single day, represented just 2.7% of fires analyzed but accounted for 78% of documented structures destroyed and 61% of reported firefighting costs. The loss is in the tail, and the tail is defined by speed and intensity, the variables of fire behavior rather than long-run frequency.

The 2026 season made the point in the field. Wind-driven fire swept into neighborhoods in Spokane, Washington, damaging more than 800 homes and forcing tens of thousands to evacuate. The underlying trajectory is worse. Under business-as-usual development and climate, structural losses are projected to nearly triple nationwide, with 133,200 to 449,100 buildings expected to be damaged or destroyed by mid-century. Averages will keep falling in quiet years while the loss-bearing events get sharper. A globally quiet fire year can still deliver a regionally catastrophic one.

Hazard is not loss

California's Fire Hazard Severity Zone maps are the most widely referenced wildfire layer in the state, and they are explicit about what they are. The maps evaluate "hazard," not "risk," based on physical conditions expected over a 30 to 50-year period without considering mitigation such as home hardening or fuel reduction. The model has two elements: probability of an area burning and expected fire behavior under extreme conditions. And the authors say the quiet part plainly: FHSZs are not a structure loss model, because key information on structure ignition, such as roof type, is not included.

That is the right design for a zoning and disclosure instrument. It is the wrong instrument for pricing a policy. A hazard class tells you a neighborhood sits in a dangerous landscape. It does not tell you whether a given home takes an ember shower, a flame-front contact, or nothing at all, and it does not resolve why one house survives while its neighbor burns. Those answers live in fire behavior at the parcel: arrival, intensity, duration, and the building's capacity to resist them.

Why the resolution matters now

The money has moved into exactly this tail. The January 2025 Los Angeles fires produced roughly $40 billion in insured losses out of about $53 billion in overall losses, and wildfire insured losses in North America are growing at an annual rate of 14%. Secondary perils, the category that includes wildfire, drove a record 92% of global insured natural catastrophe losses in 2025. These are no longer the perils you model loosely.

Most models are judged on history. We also grade ours forward.

The regulatory door has opened to match. California now allows forward-looking catastrophe models in rate filings, which rewards tools that estimate future loss rather than replay claims history. A hazard zone cannot carry that weight on its own.
