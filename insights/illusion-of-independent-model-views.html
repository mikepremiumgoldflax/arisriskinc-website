The illusion of independent model views

A specialist reinsurance broker just added another wildfire model to its shelf, framing it as an additional, independent view of risk. That instinct — more views, more safety — is the industry's standard response to a peril it doesn't trust itself to model. On a fat-tailed peril priced at parcel scale, it is the wrong reflex, and the reasons are worth spelling out for anyone deploying capital on model output.

More views, less diversity

When a peril is hard to model, the market's reflex is to buy another opinion. A specialist reinsurance broker recently licensed a wildfire model explicitly to incorporate additional, independent views of risk. The same firm's head of catastrophe analytics was candid that wildfire has often faced criticism regarding validation against experience and the treatment of extreme tail-risk scenarios. That honesty is welcome. The remedy — stack another view on top — is where the logic quietly breaks.

Independence in the statistical sense is what makes multiple estimates worth having: errors that do not move together average out. But the wildfire models on offer are not independent where it counts. They lean on the same short catalog of validation events. The model just licensed was validated against the 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires — the year's costliest event, which Moody's ranked the second- and third-most damaging wildfires in California's history, with over 18,000 structures destroyed across more than 37,000 acres. Real events, real data. But two realizations of a fat-tailed process tell you almost nothing about the tail.

Two fires are not a distribution. Calibrating to the disaster that already happened is not the same as being ready for the one that has not.

Correlated where it hurts

Shared validation events are only half the problem. The larger correlation is structural. Most catalog models frame wildfire as a wildland phenomenon and resolve exposure coarsely, which systematically understates the mechanism that now drives urban loss: conflagration, fire moving structure to structure. Cotality's own analysis warns that traditional models emphasizing terrain and vegetation can understate this neighborhood-level hazard, adding as many as 40 points to a property's risk score. If several supposedly independent models share that blind spot, averaging them does not cancel the error — it launders it into false confidence.

The resolution problem compounds it. Guidewire HazardHub found that neighboring properties within the same wildfire-prone ZIP code can differ by more than fourfold in risk, and that roughly 98% of wildfire property damage in California occurs within about 10% of the state's highest-risk properties. A model that resolves to ZIP or county cannot see that concentration; a second model at the same resolution cannot see it either. Two blind maps do not make a sighted one.

Moody's post-event reconnaissance from the Los Angeles fires makes the point physically. Homes were saved or lost on the strength of defensible ridgelines, irrigated grounds, and fire-resistant structures, while many homes untouched by flame were rendered uninhabitable by smoke. Those are parcel-scale and structure-scale facts. A view of risk that cannot represent them is not a different opinion — it is the same coarse opinion in a new wrapper.

What actual independence looks like

This matters because the capital riding on these views is enormous. California's insurer of last resort now carries roughly $768 billion in exposure and is raising rates an average of 29.1% on October 15. Pricing, reinsurance structuring, and solvency judgments all run on model output. The question to ask a vendor is not whether this is another view, but whether it is independent in a way that actually shrinks your error.

Genuine independence has three tests. First, out-of-sample in time: a forecast committed before the season it describes, so reality grades it rather than a recalibration. Second, resolution fine enough to capture the parcel-level concentration the loss data insists on. Third, a mechanism grounded in physics — fuel, terrain, wind, defensible space, structure hardening — rather than a curve fit to the last big fire.

That is the standard ARIS builds to. Our 2026 California forecast was sealed and cryptographically timestamped before the season, so it is a true forward test, not a backfit. It runs at parcel resolution on physics-grounded fire science, and it is bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable for data leakage. As a pure-play data firm that does not write insurance, we have no book to defend. Buying a second view is easy. Buying a genuinely independent one is the harder, and far more valuable, thing.
