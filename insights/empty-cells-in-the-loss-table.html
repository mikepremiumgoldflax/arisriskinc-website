The empty cells in the loss table

Actuaries trust loss history, and for most perils that trust is earned. Wildfire is the exception that should keep you up at night. The fires that break a book are increasingly the ones that strike where nothing has burned before, in neighborhoods a claims table rates as quiet and a legacy map rates as safe. When your best predictor is silent exactly where the exposure concentrates, you are not pricing risk. You are pricing memory.

The signal lives where the record is blank

The industry keeps rediscovering this the hard way. Catastrophe reporting in 2025 flagged a record number of fire-related catastrophe declarations, including events in provinces with no prior industry fire catastrophes. A loss model fed on history had nothing to say about those places, because the cells in the table were empty. Empty cells are not evidence of safety. They are evidence of ignorance, and in wildfire they tend to be where the next concentrated loss lands.

The urgent need is for forward-looking wildfire intelligence able to highlight locations that don't have prior fire losses.

The built environment makes the blank cells worse. When wind-driven embers cross into a developed neighborhood, homes and fences become the fuel, and a wildland fire becomes an urban conflagration. Cotality's own analysis puts a number on the blind spot: layering conflagration potential onto a traditional score can add as many as 40 points to a property's score, pushing meaningful hazard deep into neighborhoods that legacy maps labeled low risk. That is not a rounding error. That is the difference between a book you think is diversified and a book that is quietly stacked.

The history you do have is thin and dated

Set aside the blank cells for a moment and look at the data that actually exists. It is startlingly sparse. Operational fire-spread estimation has long leaned on 15 to 20 prescribed burns from the 1980s, plus weather and forest data, fused with decades of human experience — a method that, in the words of the researchers trying to replace it, hasn't been adapted to current fire severity increases due to climate change and involved a great deal of extrapolation from a limited dataset. Even state-of-the-art data collection remains tiny by machine-learning standards; a single season of 50 logged fires counted as the highest number recorded.

This is the uncomfortable truth under a lot of wildfire pricing. The record is both too short and too old to describe a hazard that is changing underneath it. The science is maturing fast — surrogate spread models now reach an F1 score of 0.92 — but the lesson of that work is physics, not precedent. You forecast where a fire will go by modeling fuel, terrain, and wind, not by waiting for a claim to prove the hazard was there.

The bill for pricing memory

California is paying that bill in public. The FAIR Plan's total exposure reached $768 billion as of June 2026, enrollment has nearly tripled, from under 2% of homes to 5%, and dwelling rates rise an average of 29.1% on October 15, 2026. The wider market is not far behind, with Cotality counting 2.5 million western homes and $1.4 trillion exposed. Much of that migration to the insurer of last resort is risk that admitted carriers could not see clearly enough to price — risk that lived in the empty cells.

The fix is not a better rear-view mirror. It is to assess the hazard directly, at the resolution the hazard actually operates. That is why ARIS builds parcel by parcel on physics — fuel, terrain, wind, and defensible space — rather than ZIP-code loss averages, and why our 2026 California forecast was sealed and cryptographically timestamped before the season, to be graded by reality instead of a backtest. Every run is bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable for leakage, and we write no insurance, so the forecast has no book to protect. The places with no loss history are exactly the ones a physics-grounded model can still see. A claims table cannot score a fire that has not happened yet. The ground, the fuel, and the wind can.
