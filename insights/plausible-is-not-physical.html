Plausible Is Not Physical

The newest pitch in catastrophe modeling is seductive: let a generative model dream up thousands of years of disasters that never happened, then price the tail off those synthetic events. The computational leverage is real. So is the failure mode nobody wants on the record. A model that has learned the texture of catastrophe can produce events that look completely convincing and are, physically, impossible. For wildfire, where the outcome at a single address turns on fuel, slope, and wind, the difference between plausible and physical is the difference between a rate that holds and one that doesn't.

The shortcut and its price

The industry has discovered that generative AI can synthetically emulate thousands of years of possible weather events in fine detail, and the appeal is obvious. Reliable weather records reach back only about a century, and the traditional catastrophe models that have been in use since the 1980s are constrained by cost and by the thinness of the historical record in the tail. If you want to understand the outlier that has not happened yet, you either wait for it or you manufacture it. Generative models manufacture it cheaply.

The cost shows up in the fine print. Even practitioners building these systems concede that AI can hallucinate and fabricate, and can produce events that seem plausible but are, in reality, at odds with physics. The recommended posture is telling: experts told the Financial Times that these outputs are best treated as informed guesses, and that human evaluation of the models is still necessary. One modeler put the value proposition plainly, calling AI a huge computational shortcut to generate the sheer number of scenarios we need to understand extremes.

A shortcut that occasionally violates the laws it is meant to respect is not a shortcut. It is a liability you have not yet priced.

Wildfire is not a texture problem

For some perils, a statistically convincing synthetic event is good enough, because the loss is driven by broad fields where small physical inconsistencies wash out. Wildfire is not that peril. The loss is decided at the parcel. Whether a structure ignites depends on the fuel touching it, the slope carrying the fire, the wind driving embers, and the defensible space that either holds or fails. A generative model that produces a fire spreading against the wind, or crowning through fuel that cannot carry it, is not conservative or aggressive. It is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that a ZIP or county aggregate will happily average into something that looks reasonable.

This is not an abstraction in July of 2026. Hot, dry, and windy conditions have driven fires across Arizona, Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico, with the Babylon Fire in Utah burning 79,795 acres at zero percent containment in early July. Every one of those perimeters was written by physics, not by a plausibility score.

What the balance sheet already knows

The tail is not hypothetical to carriers either. After the January 2025 Los Angeles County fires, the California FAIR Plan absorbed roughly $4 billion in claims and needed a $1-billion bailout from its member insurers, and a court has now upheld the surcharges passed on to policyholders to cover part of it. When the tail arrives, someone pays it in real dollars. A model whose extreme scenarios are informed guesses is a model whose capital charge is an informed guess.

Grounding beats guessing

The right response is not to reject computation. It is to refuse to let plausibility stand in for physics. A wildfire scenario should be constrained by the same fuel, terrain, and wind mechanics that govern a real fire, so that an impossible event cannot be generated in the first place. And every scenario should be reproducible and auditable, so that a reviewer can check bit-for-bit how a number was produced and confirm that the model has not quietly learned the answer from data it should never have seen.
