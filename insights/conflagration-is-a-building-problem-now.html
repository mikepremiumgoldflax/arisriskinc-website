Conflagration is a building problem now

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires did not merely set loss records. They changed what kind of peril wildfire is. The destruction that mattered most did not happen out in unmapped brush. It happened block by block, as one burning structure ignited the next. That is not a wildland vegetation problem. It is a building-density, parcel-geometry, ember-pathway problem, and it belongs to a scale of analysis that ZIP- and county-level catastrophe models were never built to resolve.

A peril class that wasn't on the spec sheet

Moody's marks January 2025 as the moment urban wildfire "became a U.S. catastrophe class of its own." The Palisades fire burned roughly 23,448 acres and the Eaton fire roughly 14,021 acres, and together the January cluster destroyed or damaged over 18,000 structures, with insured losses landing in the US$25 to $30 billion range. The Eaton fire alone left more than 9,400 structures destroyed and over 1,000 damaged.

What drove the severity was not a mystery of ignition location. Moody's reconnaissance identified connective fuels, older construction such as single-pane windows, ember pathways, and structure-to-structure spread under high winds as the mechanics of loss. Early findings from the IBHS described a textbook worst-case coupling of wind vectors and street orientation, turning neighborhoods into fuel rather than breaks. Once a fire enters the wildland-urban interface, the dominant fuel is the built environment itself.

The science moved. The models that price the risk did not.

Fire science has advanced on exactly the variables that decide which structures burn. USC Viterbi researchers have shown that ignition time and terrain slope and elevation materially change the accuracy of spread prediction. Researchers at the FAMU-FSU College of Engineering are attacking a different blind spot, noting that current fire models oversimplify forests as static blocks and fail to capture how vegetation actually interacts with wind and flame. The frontier of the field is getting more granular, more physical, and more local, even as U.S. wildfire activity has approximately doubled over two decades, with annual economic impacts now in the hundreds of billions.

Meanwhile, the models that set price are being institutionalized at a coarser grain. California's Sustainable Insurance Strategy incorporates CDI-approved catastrophe modeling and the net cost of reinsurance into rate filings, and the FAIR Plan just used it to justify a 29.1% overall rate increase effective October 15. The stakes are not abstract. The FAIR Plan's book grew to 696,560 policies and roughly $768 billion in exposure as of June 30. When the peril has shifted to a parcel-scale conflagration process, the resolution of the model that prices it is not a technical footnote. It is the whole question.

A model that averages risk across a ZIP code cannot tell you that the house with a clear defensible space survived while its neighbor, twenty meters away and up a slope, did not. In a conflagration, that twenty meters is the entire story.

This is why the industry conversation is turning toward granularity. The most recent western exposure estimates put 2.5 million homes and $1.4 trillion at risk, and explicitly frame property-level data as the requirement, not the luxury.
