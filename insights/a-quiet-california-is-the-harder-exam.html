A Quiet California Is the Harder Exam

A loud fire year flatters almost any risk model. A quiet one exposes it. In 2026 the United States burned far above its long-run average, yet California — the state everyone watches — stayed unusually calm. That split is not a reprieve. It is the hardest exam a forecast can sit, because it asks a question averages cannot answer: not how much burned, but exactly where.

Acres are not losses

Start with the number everyone quotes. By mid-September 2026, more than 8.5 million acres had burned across the United States, about 146% of the ten-year average, with the country spending 49 consecutive days at the highest national preparedness level. Read alone, that total tells you the system was stretched. It tells you almost nothing about where the money went.

Look underneath and the averages come apart. Oregon set a state acreage record of roughly 2.56 million acres while California ran at about 38% of its five-year average — in the same season. And acreage was never a good proxy for loss in the first place. The January 2025 Los Angeles fires burned roughly 37,500 acres yet produced an estimated $53 billion in damage. A season can be small by area and ruinous by dollars, because loss is governed by what sits in the fire's path and how vulnerable it is — not by how many square miles of brush ignited.

Why the quiet year is the sterner test

Here is the uncomfortable part for anyone selling catastrophe intelligence. A model that smears risk across ZIP or county geographies, or that leans heavily on recent frequency, gets flattered by a loud year and humiliated by a quiet one. Flag everything, and a bad fire season makes you look prescient. The quiet year strips that cover away. It asks whether you called the specific parcels that burned, and — just as demanding — whether you correctly declined to flag the ones that did not.

A statewide average can be wrong in both directions at once: too high for the county that stayed green all summer, too low for the street that burned to its foundations.

Resolution is what separates the two. Cotality's 2026 report puts weight on conflagration — fire moving structure to structure through a built neighborhood rather than burning as contained wildland. Adding that factor can raise a property's wildfire score by as many as 40 points, pushing meaningful hazard into neighborhoods legacy maps had classified as low risk. The hazard was always there. The coarse map simply could not see it. A quiet year is where that blindness shows, because the damage concentrates in a handful of exposures that a county-level view averages into invisibility.

Physics is how you earn the resolution

You do not get to parcel-level precision by drawing smoother polygons. You get there by modeling how fire actually behaves. USC researchers advancing a wildfire prediction model note that ignition timing and terrain — how slope and elevation drive the direction and speed of spread — materially change where a fire goes, and that fire tends to run faster uphill. One of the lead researchers lived the stakes: he was evacuated during the Eaton Fire, which destroyed more than 9,400 structures. Fuel, wind, slope, and the condition of the parcel itself are the variables that decide which house stands. A score built on those is defensible in a quiet year and a loud one. A score built on historical frequency is only ever as good as its luck.

What reality will grade

This is why ARIS sealed and cryptographically timestamped its 2026 California forecast before the season, at parcel resolution. A calm California does not let that forecast off the hook — it sharpens the test. Reality grades it where it matters: on the specific parcels that burned and the ones that did not, not on a statewide average that can hide both errors at once. In a quiet year, that is the only kind of claim worth making.
