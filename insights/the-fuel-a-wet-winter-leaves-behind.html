The fuel a wet winter leaves behind

A bad fire season does not start with an ignition. It starts with the water that fell the winter before, the biomass that water grew, and the structure of the fuel that was waiting when the heat arrived. If you want to price what will burn, you have to model the thing that does the burning, and that thing changes from year to year.

Three ingredients, and only one of them is weather

Fire needs an ignition, weather that favors spread, and fuel to sustain it. Terrain does the rest, pre-heating fuel ahead of the flame and bending the wind through valleys and slopes. Under extreme conditions the fire builds its own weather, with rising currents that make spread unpredictable. Underwriters tend to fixate on the weather ingredient because it is the one the news covers. It is also the one that resets every few days. The ingredient that carries memory from season to season is fuel.

Western Europe this summer is the clean illustration. The area hit by the Avila-Madrid-Toledo fire had seen rainfall roughly 175% above normal, following the wettest January in Spain in a quarter century. That water did not make the landscape safe. It grew biomass. When heat and drought cured that biomass, the Avila fire burned about 44,000 hectares and became the largest in Spain's recorded history. A wet winter wrote the fire season that followed.

Fuel structure, not just fuel load

How much fuel there is matters. How it is arranged matters more. Near Bordeaux, the fire ran through over 800,000 hectares of intensively managed maritime pine, with needle litter on the ground, ladder fuels connecting ground to canopy, and connected crowns that let the fire climb and jump. The result was a crown fire that generated its own thunderstorm and spread fast enough to make the Gironde blaze the second largest single wildfire in France since the Second World War. Same drought, same heat, different fuel geometry would have produced a different fire.

A hazard score is a photograph. Fuel is a film. The question is not how dangerous a place looks today, but how the ingredients will combine the next time the weather turns.

This is the structural problem with a static hazard layer. It freezes a landscape that is in motion. Vegetation grows, dries, recovers, and is thinned or left alone. A parcel that scored moderate three years ago may sit downhill from a slope that has since loaded with cured fuel. A map that cannot see that change cannot price it.

Why this is an accumulation problem, not just a parcel problem

The dollars make the case for getting the physics right. One industry estimate puts roughly 2.5 million western homes and about $1.4 trillion of value in the path of wildfire. In California, the insurer of last resort reported total exposure of $768 billion as of June 2026, a 250% jump since September 2022, against a cash balance between $200 and $400 million, and is raising rates an average of 29.1% this fall. Concentration like that is a fuel-structure question dressed up as a balance-sheet question. When one wind event lines up with cured, connected fuel across a region, the correlated losses are what break a book.

So the modeling task is not to label a place dangerous. It is to simulate how fire reaches a given building and how hard it hits, given this year's fuel, this terrain, and the weather that could plausibly show up. That is what ARIS does: a physics-based wildfire model, building by building, run across a ten-thousand-season catalogue, with mitigation planning and rescoring on the same engine and portfolio and accumulation views built on top. When fuel changes, the model changes. Most models are judged on history. The fuel a wet winter leaves behind is a measurable thing. It should be priced like one.
