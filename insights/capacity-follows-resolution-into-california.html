Capacity follows resolution into California

For three years the California story was about who was leaving. It has quietly flipped. The insurer of last resort is raising rates across the board because it can only average, while a handful of carriers are entering the hardest market in the country because they can tell one parcel from its neighbor. That contrast is the whole argument for resolution, and it is showing up in filings, not slides.

The insurer of last resort can only average

The California FAIR Plan is raising dwelling rates about 29.1% effective October 15, 2026, with the largest component tied to the wildfire portion of premium. That is what socialized pricing looks like when you cannot differentiate. When your book is defined as the risk nobody else wanted, and your tools resolve risk coarsely, you have one lever: raise the average and let the properties at genuine risk pull the mean up for everyone. The FAIR Plan says some policyholders will actually see a decrease, but the direction of travel is a blunt, portfolio-wide correction rather than a parcel-by-parcel one.

This is not a knock on the FAIR Plan. It is a structural fact. An entity that absorbs the residual market and prices at a coarse grain has no way to reward the hardened home surrounded by defensible space over the tinderbox two lots away. Averaging is the only move left.

Carriers that can differentiate are entering

Meanwhile, capacity is moving the other way. Three carriers just launched or expanded California homeowners programs on the back of a property-level wildfire model, with DUAL North America, Kingstone and Windward each underwriting parcel by parcel. This is happening despite the January 2025 Los Angeles fires, where the Eaton and Palisades events destroyed more than 16,200 structures and generated an estimated $40 billion in insured losses, the largest insured wildfire loss on record per the Swiss Re Institute. The reason these carriers can enter a market that size is precision: only 11% of California homes fall into the High Risk tier in that model's 2025 review, which means the other 89% is writable if you can see it clearly.

The California story has been about who was leaving. The carriers now entering are writing a different one, each with a property-by-property view of risk. That precision is what lets capacity stay.

Regulation is the enabler. Under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, carriers can adopt forward-looking catastrophe models in exchange for writing at-risk policies equal to at least 85% of their market share. But the rate level itself stays capped: Farmers first requested 6.99% and was approved at just 1.5%. When the regulator holds the aggregate rate down, the only way to write profitably is to select well. Differentiation migrates from the rate filing to the risk selection, and selection is a resolution problem.

Resolution is where the risk actually lives

The case for fine grain is not aesthetic. In a parcel-level analysis, more than 2.5 million properties across ten states carry moderate or greater wildfire risk, with a combined reconstruction cost approaching $1.4 trillion — and adding structure-to-structure spread can push a single property's score up by as many as 40 points, deep into neighborhoods legacy assessments called low risk. Coarse models step risk down as development gets denser; the fire does the opposite once it reaches homes.

The physics reinforces the point. Recent USC work shows that ignition time and terrain — slope and elevation — materially change a fire's predicted path, intensity, and growth rate. Spread is governed by fuel, wind, and ground truth that vary lot to lot. A model that resolves at ZIP or county cannot represent any of it.

When averaging is a losing strategy and selection is the game, the carriers who see each parcel clearly are the ones who get to stay. Resolution is now a capacity decision.
