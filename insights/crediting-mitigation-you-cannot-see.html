MitigationAugust 2026

Crediting mitigation you cannot see

The new California rules are usually described as permission to look forward. That undersells them. They also impose an obligation: to price the fuel break, the ember-resistant roof, the cleared defensible space around a specific structure. Colorado has gone further and made the score something a homeowner can contest. Both moves quietly demand something most catastrophe models were never built to deliver — a view of risk at the resolution where mitigation actually happens.

The rules now reward what the map can't see

California's overhaul does two things at once. It lets carriers use forward-looking modeling that incorporates the effects of climate change when setting rates, and it conditions that privilege on accounting for mitigation taken by homeowners, businesses and communities. The regulator was blunt about why the old regime failed: a historical approach makes it impossible to account for how mitigation — such as nearby firebreaks or prescribed burns — may lessen future risk.

The stakes behind that sentence are not abstract. California's insurer of last resort is raising rates 29.1% starting October 15, with the wildfire portion driving the largest share of the increase. When the price of a policy turns on the wildfire component, the model that estimates that component has to be able to see, and credit, the things that change it.

Mitigation is a parcel fact, not a ZIP fact

The Los Angeles fires made the resolution problem impossible to ignore. Post-event reconnaissance found that defensible ridgelines, fire-resistant structures, and even irrigated cemetery grounds served as critical firebreaks, often sparing entire neighborhoods, while ember accumulators, connected fuels, and high-density building layouts amplified the destruction. The two largest fires still destroyed over 18,000 structures, but the line between the homes that burned and the ones that didn't was drawn at the scale of a roofline, a cleared lot, a slope — not a postal code.

That is the crux. Mitigation is a property-level and feature-level fact. A model that resolves risk to a ZIP or county cannot, even in principle, distinguish the hardened home from the tinderbox next door, because it has averaged them together before it ever reached a rate. You cannot give credit for something you cannot see, and you cannot see a fuel break from thirty thousand feet.

If the regulation rewards mitigation but the model can't resolve it, the credit becomes arbitrary — and arbitrary credits are exactly what an appeals process is designed to expose.

An appealable number changes the burden of proof

Colorado has raised the bar from pricing to accountability. Its new law requires insurers to be transparent about their wildfire mitigation risk models and allows property owners to appeal their scores. Once a homeowner can demand to know why their score is what it is — and challenge it — a coarse model becomes a liability. "Your ZIP code is high risk" is not a defensible answer to someone who has spent thousands clearing brush and replacing a roof. The model now has to explain itself at the parcel.

The vendor market senses the shift. One recent launch concedes that traditional approaches often rely on historical loss data and static hazard maps that may not fully capture today's changing wildfire conditions, and pairs physics-based fire behavior with wildfire spread modeling at 30-metre resolution across the continental United States. The direction of travel is clear: away from historical averages and toward the physical processes that actually decide whether a structure survives.

What this demands of a model

A framework that credits mitigation and defends the credit needs three properties at once: resolution fine enough to see a single parcel, physics grounded enough to explain why fuel, terrain, wind, and defensible space produce the score they do, and an audit trail robust enough to survive an appeal. That is the standard ARIS builds to. Our forecasts are parcel-level and physics-grounded rather than aggregated to ZIP or county, so a hardened home and its vulnerable neighbor get different answers. They are bit-for-bit reproducible and auditable for data leakage, so a score can be reconstructed and defended line by line. And because ARIS does not write insurance, there is no channel conflict shaping the number. The 2026 California forecast is sealed and cryptographically timestamped, which means the market — not a backtest — gets to decide whether the resolution was real.
