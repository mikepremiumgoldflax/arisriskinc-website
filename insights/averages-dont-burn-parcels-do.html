Averages don't burn. Parcels do.

Every fall a big round number lands on executive desks and gets treated as the state of the peril. This year the number is a record industry catastrophe-loss benchmark. It is a useful capital figure and a terrible underwriting one, because averages don't burn houses. Parcels do, and wildfire loss is concentrated in a way that a portfolio average is designed to hide.

A capital number wearing an underwriting hat

The 2026 industry benchmark puts average annual insured catastrophe losses at a record $171 billion, up $19 billion from the prior year and the sixth consecutive year above $100 billion. What is striking is the stated cause. The rise is attributed to rising property values, higher reconstruction costs, and expansion into hazard-prone areas rather than any single severe season, in a year with no U.S. hurricane landfalls. It is also a broad number: severe thunderstorms are the single largest contributor at 40% of the modeled risk.

A benchmark like this answers a capital question: how much loss should a balance sheet be able to absorb across a diversified book. It cannot answer the question an underwriter actually faces, which is whether to bind this address, at this price, with this deductible. Aggregate numbers are built to smooth. Wildfire underwriting requires the opposite.

Where the loss actually lives

Wildfire is not smooth. It is one of the most concentrated perils in property insurance. Recent parcel-level analysis classifies more than one million California homes as very high risk, with roughly 98% of wildfire-related property damage occurring within about 10% of the state's highest-risk properties. On top of that concentration, the variance inside a single postal code is enormous: research found that neighboring properties within the same wildfire-prone ZIP code can differ by more than fourfold in overall risk.

A portfolio average and a ZIP-code lookup make the same mistake in opposite directions. One tells you the peril is everywhere; the other tells you it is uniform across a street. Neither is where the fire goes.

The industry's own literature now concedes the deeper problem. Wildfire has shifted from an attritional peril to a catastrophic one, and historical experience alone no longer reliably predicts future risk as fire seasons lengthen and development pushes further into the wildland-urban interface. When the past stops being a stable guide, an actuarial frequency-severity view built on loss history is estimating the wrong distribution.

You can see the pressure in the market plumbing. California's insurer of last resort reached 696,562 policies in force and $768 billion in exposure by June 30, 2026, up roughly 250% since September 2022, and its rates were approved for a 29.1% average statewide increase effective October 15, while statewide homeowners premiums are up 84% since 2020. That is what happens when the market cannot tell adjacent risks apart: it retreats in blocks instead of pricing homes.

Resolution that matches the peril

The good news is that the peril is physically legible at the level where it matters. Mitigation research shows that homes with well-maintained defensible space were 65% less likely to suffer significant damage, ember-resistant vents cut ignition likelihood by 40%, and combined measures reduced expected losses by up to 70%. Those are parcel attributes, invisible to any county or ZIP average. Fire science is moving the same direction: researchers now improve prediction by pinning down ignition timing from geostationary satellite data and accounting for how slope and terrain govern spread, because fuel, wind, and topography are what actually decide which structure survives.
