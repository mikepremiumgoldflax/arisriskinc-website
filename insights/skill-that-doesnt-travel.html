The 0.92 that collapsed to 0.75 one kilometer over

A model that scores brilliantly on a random test split can be worthless the moment you move it to a neighborhood it has never seen. A new analysis of the Palisades Fire makes the point in one brutal number, and it is the number every actuary buying a wildfire model should ask about first.

A number that looked like skill

A new study of the January 2025 Palisades Fire is a quiet warning for anyone who buys or builds structure-loss models. The authors linked 12,081 CAL FIRE damage inspections to pre-fire vegetation, surface temperature, fuels, ten-meter terrain and building footprints, then modeled which homes burned — 5,566 of 9,883 inspected residential structures were destroyed. Under random cross-validation, their gradient-boosting model posted a ROC-AUC of 0.92, which reads as excellent discrimination. Then they re-ran the test under spatial block validation, holding out whole one-kilometer tiles the model had never trained on. Performance fell to 0.75.

Same data, same features, same event. The only thing that changed was whether the test homes sat next door to the training homes. Most of the apparent skill was the model recognizing its own neighborhoods, not learning how fire destroys a house.

Why spatial generalization is the real test

Random splits let a model peek across the street. Homes a block apart share weather, slope, vegetation and construction era, so a model tested on a random hold-out is effectively quizzed on streets it has already studied. Block validation severs that tie and asks the only question that matters for a book of business: does this travel to ground you have not seen. In the Palisades work it mostly did not. The honest finding is that an interpretable logistic model performed about as well under spatial blocking and was better calibrated — the fancier model's edge was an illusion of the test design.

What did carry real signal is instructive. Neighborhood building count was the strongest predictor, with destruction odds rising roughly fourfold per standard deviation in buildings within 100 meters, and the information concentrated at the 100-to-300-meter neighborhood scale. That is not a vegetation story. It is a story about structures igniting structures. The authors are blunt about what their approach can and cannot do:

The results position open geospatial modeling as a neighborhood-scale screening tool rather than a parcel-level prediction system.

This matches what we saw on the ground elsewhere in 2026. In the Spokane Complex Fire, which destroyed more than 800 homes, the damage was driven by fire entering the wildland-urban interface and then propagating from one structure to the next. Once buildings become fuel, you are no longer modeling a landscape. You are modeling a chain of ignitions through a built fabric, and statistical fits calibrated to one town's street grid have no particular reason to describe another's.

What generalizes is physics

A correlation learned on one neighborhood is a hostage to that neighborhood. The mechanism — radiant heat, ember flux, the geometry of structure-to-structure spread — is the same everywhere. That is the case for a physics-based approach rather than a pattern fit: you solve for when fire reaches a home and how hard it hits, building by building, and the solution does not depend on having already seen the answer for the block next door.
