Your Risk Score Is Now a Legal Object

For most of the history of catastrophe modeling, a wildfire risk score was a private number. It moved premiums, it moved appetite, and no homeowner ever saw the machinery behind it. That era is ending. Regulators are turning the score into something a property owner can read, question, and formally contest — and that changes what a model has to be able to do. It is no longer enough to be roughly right across a portfolio. You now have to be able to defend a single house.

The score became something you have to explain

Colorado made the shift explicit. Under HB25-1182, which took effect July 1, 2026, insurers must be transparent about the wildfire mitigation risk models they use and property owners can appeal their scores. The law is not a suggestion to be nicer. It requires a written notice with a plain-language explanation of the risk score, a range of possible scores a property could receive, and the specific impact each mitigation action could have. It puts the burden of proof on the carrier and it runs a clock: the insurer must acknowledge an appeal within 10 calendar days and issue a reconsideration within 30.

Crucially, the same law requires insurers to consider parcel-level and community-wide mitigation so that risk scores reflect the property and the surrounding area. That is a resolution mandate hiding inside a transparency mandate. You cannot credit a homeowner for clearing brush near a structure if your model does not see the structure.

You cannot decompose a ZIP code

This is where legacy resolution becomes a liability rather than a modeling nuance. When a homeowner appeals and asks why their hardened house scored like the untended lot down the street, a county- or ZIP-level model has no honest answer. It never distinguished the two houses in the first place. The score was an average dressed up as a measurement, and averages do not survive cross-examination. A model that cannot be decomposed to the individual parcel cannot explain a parcel-level decision, cannot defend it in an appeal, and cannot be trusted to reward mitigation the law now says must be rewarded.

The physical evidence points the same direction. In post-event reconnaissance after the 2025 Los Angeles fires, Moody's teams found that defensible ridgelines and fire-resistant structures served as critical firebreaks that spared entire neighborhoods, while ember accumulators, connected fuels, and high-density layouts amplified the destruction. Outcomes were decided at the scale of fuel, wind path, and building detail — not at the scale of a postal boundary. Reviewers described a textbook coupling of wind vectors and street orientation that turned neighborhoods into fuel rather than breaks. That is a parcel-and-terrain story from start to finish.

A score that cannot survive an appeal is not intelligence. It is an assertion waiting to be overturned.

California is pushing toward the same test

California is arriving at the accountability problem from the pricing side. The FAIR Plan's 29.1% rate increase effective October 15, 2026 was filed under the Sustainable Insurance Strategy, which incorporates CDI-approved catastrophe modeling, and the plan explicitly notes that policyholders may qualify for discounts by hardening their home. Once a discount is tied to a specific mitigation action, the model has to see that action on a specific property. The pressure is not academic. The FAIR Plan's book grew 44% to more than 668,600 policies with total exposure of $724 billion at the end of 2025. Every one of those parcels is a potential dispute about whether the risk was measured or merely assumed.

What this means for the model you buy

The regulatory trend is clear: scores that were once opaque are becoming contestable, explainable, and auditable by law. A model built for that world has to resolve to the individual parcel, has to ground its numbers in the physics that actually decides outcomes — fuel, terrain, wind, and defensible space — and has to reproduce its own reasoning on demand rather than gesture at a proprietary black box.
